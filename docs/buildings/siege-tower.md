# Medieval European siege tower: references and circulation

## Sources inspected

- [Wikipedia, Siege tower (Japanese)](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/攻城塔): identifies the diagram and its context; this is a starting point, not a construction specification.
- [Viollet-le-Duc, *Dictionnaire raisonné*, vol. 5, “Engin”, fig. 32 and accompanying text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_raisonn%C3%A9_de_l%E2%80%99architecture_fran%C3%A7aise_du_XIe_au_XVIe_si%C3%A8cle/Engin). The drawing is a nineteenth-century interpretation of medieval equipment, not a contemporary medieval technical drawing. The text describes four wheels, internal stairs, an almost open rear, a bridge lowered onto the wall, and archers on the level above the assault platform. It explicitly says the planks and hides were omitted from the drawing to reveal its frame.
- [Full-resolution fig. 32, “Beffroi d'assaut”](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Beffroi.d.assaut.png): visually checked the transverse ladder rungs, successive offsets of the ascents, the bridge H and the higher shooting level I.
- [Viollet-le-Duc, vol. 2, “Beffroi, machine de guerre”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/30782/pg30782-images.html): describes multiple protected floors and fresh hides/wet wool, and notes the limited surviving practical information. Its account reinforces that exact mechanisms cannot be treated as established construction drawings.

## Interpretation used in BV

A short rear boarding ladder connects the ground to the elevated chassis deck; its exact construction is also an interpretation.

The model follows the reference's circulation principles, with illustrative dimensions and four decks rather than a literal copy of every level and groundwork in fig. 32. Positive X faces the castle wall. The three ladders climb across the tower along positive Z, with their rungs along X. Their X positions alternate, so the upper exit is not directly under the next ladder. An incoming soldier steps onto a landing beyond the hatch, moves sideways, and approaches the next ladder in a separate lane. The assault deck has a route from its landing directly to the bridge; the ascent to the archer deck stays behind that route.

Deck openings are real gaps in both decking and supporting members. Their rear extent allows a standing-height clearance envelope to pass the upper deck while climbing. Headers and trimmers frame the gaps; guard rails run along their sides, leaving the landing end open. These guards, exact offsets and hatch dimensions are modeling decisions, not dimensions transcribed from the historical drawing.

The former waist-high transverse winding shaft blocked the assault route. It is replaced by two small longitudinal windlasses in the wall-side strips, with ropes and sheaves outside the bridge's walking width. The paired mechanism and its small crank/ratchet details are plausible modeling interpretations, not specifically attested by fig. 32. The threshold and bridge have matching walking surfaces, with hinge fittings outside the central passage. Bridge length is limited to fit below the upper floor when raised. The static lowered bridge represents the assault position resting on an implied castle wall, as described in the source; it is not a cantilever solely supported by the illustrated ropes.

The archer platform is above the assault doorway and has an open crenellated top in place of the previous pitched canopy. Protective boarding and hides remain on the front and sides of the lower enclosed levels. An open rear makes the internal circulation visible, consistent with the reference's description.

## Validation scope

Regression checks cover ground contact, ladder orientation, offset ascent lanes, clearance through the actual deck openings and their framing, landing/approach clearance, an unobstructed assault corridor, matching bridge/threshold levels, and clearance for the raised bridge beneath the upper deck. Resource disposal and generated projections are also checked. These are geometric circulation checks, not structural load analysis, a crowd simulation, or a claim that every medieval siege tower shared this arrangement.
